# Farmington Canal: Historic Route, Maps & Remains

The **Farmington Canal** was the Connecticut section of the waterway connecting New Haven with Northampton, Massachusetts. It carried passengers and freight inland from New Haven harbor, continuing at the state line as the [Hampshire & Hampden Canal](/hampshire-and-hampden-canal). Together, the two sections became the New Haven and Northampton Canal system. [Connecticut History's canal overview](https://connecticuthistory.org/the-farmington-canal/) explains the companies and the connection between the states.

This guide follows the historic waterway through Connecticut. Use the [interactive Farmington Canal map](#canal-map) to compare its reconstructed route with today's landscape, locate locks and feeders, and examine terrain through Lidar imagery.

## Where did the Farmington Canal run?

From south to north, explore the canal through [New Haven](/towns/new-haven), [Hamden](/towns/hamden), [Cheshire](/towns/cheshire), [Southington](/towns/southington), [Plainville](/towns/plainville), [Farmington](/towns/farmington), [Avon](/towns/avon), [Simsbury](/towns/simsbury), and [Granby](/towns/granby). These are also the nine towns covered by [Carl Walter's Farmington Canal map series, published by Simsbury Free Library](https://simsburyfreelibrary.org/book-store/farmington-canal-maps/).

The town histories describe how the canal changed local commerce and how its builders dealt with water supply, changes in elevation, and river crossings. The map includes feeder canals as well as the main line: follow these branches to understand how water reached the navigation canal.

## When was the canal built and used?

| Date | Milestone |
| --- | --- |
| 1822 | Connecticut chartered the Farmington Canal Company. |
| 1825 | Construction began. |
| 1828 | The New Haven–Farmington section opened to traffic. |
| 1835 | The through route reached Northampton in Massachusetts. |
| Late 1840s | The canal era gave way to railroad development. |

The canal opened in stages, so the opening date for a particular town differs from the completion date for the whole system. Sources: [Connecticut History on the canal's construction](https://connecticuthistory.org/farmington-canal-designed-to-give-connecticut-commerce-a-competitive-edge/) and [New Haven's transition from canal to railroad](https://connecticuthistory.org/new-havens-long-wharf/).

## Is this the same as the Farmington Canal Heritage Trail?

The historic canal and the modern recreational trail are related, but serve different purposes. This site's map reconstructs the nineteenth-century waterway; it is not a current cycling or walking itinerary. Connecticut State Parks describes the [Farmington Canal Greenway State Park](https://ctparks.com/parks/farmington-canal-greenway-state-park) in Cheshire and Hamden as part of the broader Farmington Canal Heritage Trail. Use the park's information for visiting that section and the [Farmington Valley Trails Council](https://fchtrail.org/) for trail resources.

## How reliable is the reconstructed route?

Some sections remain legible in the terrain; others have been altered or erased. This project combines Lidar, historical maps, and aerial photography, rather than treating the entire line as a surviving canal bed. Read [how the canal was mapped](/about) for the method and [Sources](/sources) for the underlying references and downloadable line data.
