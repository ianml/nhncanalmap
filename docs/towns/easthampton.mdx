# Canal Towns: Easthampton

Citizens from Southampton were involved in the planning meetings for the Hampshire and Hampden Canal as early as 1822 and 1825. The town was also a meeting place for the canal corporation, hosting the annual stockholders' meeting in January 1827 at the house of Apollas Metcalf. The engineering estimate for the canal section through Southampton, prepared by Mr. Hurd, included costs for excavation, embankments, locks, and bridges totaling $33,338. In January 1833, the company formally sought proposals for constructing the "trunks" for aqueducts in the town, specifically mentioning the one over the Manhan River near Strong's Mills.

The canal was officially opened for navigation as far north as Southampton by July 1, 1835 , and just a week later, a boat carrying freight successfully traveled to the town and returned. Southampton became a regular stop for both freight and passenger service. Gaius Lyman served as the Collector for the canal office in Southampton, clearing manifests for boats like the "Old Tip" and "Ceres" in 1841. Numerous invoices from 1838 and 1839, preserved by the Southampton Historical Society, detail the active commerce, showing freight like salt, flour, codfish, sugar, and tea being shipped from New Haven to local merchants like Gaius Lyman Jr. and R. E. Thorp on the boats *Ceres,* *Flora*, and *Venus*. The canal also spurred other local industries; in 1840, a resident inquired about leasing water power from the "second lock" in Southampton to run a mill and a brick yard.

Despite this commercial activity, the canal's route through Southampton was plagued by structural failures. The waterway suffered multiple breaches that suspended navigation. One significant break occurred in the southern part of Southampton in December 1834, washing away eight to ten rods of embankment just as the full line to Northampton was nearing completion. This incident left a canal boat, along with its passengers, stranded in the mud. Additional breaks near Southampton were reported in July 1839 and August 1842. The local rivers also posed a threat; a major freshet in 1826 reportedly carried away eight bridges on the Manhan River in Southampton.

In addition to freight, packet boats provided regular passenger service through the town. A passenger recalling a seven-day journey from Northampton to New Haven in 1835 specifically mentioned passing through "quiet, old Southampton". The New Haven Packet Boat Company advertised daily service on the *Doe*, *Hart*, and *Fawn*, listing Southampton as a scheduled stop with a fare of $3.37 from New Haven. The town also contributed to the canal's fleet, as a report from 1835 noted that a new canal boat was being built in Southampton.
